## Why does selling a business feel so emotional?

For most founders this is their first exit. There is no previous sale to compare it with and no sense of how it will feel. And the thing being sold is not just an asset. It is the company you started, carried and raised for years. Many founders call it their baby, and they mean it.

In the years before a sale, the business often gets the blame for everything that feels heavy: the headaches, the responsibility, the burnout. Some of that is fair. But something surprising happens after the exit. The responsibility and the daily problem-solving that founders wanted to get rid of turn out to be the very things they miss. The business was the source of the stress, and it was also the source of the drive.

Knowing this in advance won't stop the feelings, but it does make them easier to recognize when they arrive.

## Before you sign: why do founders get cold feet?

Anxiety before a sale is normal. The obvious fear is about price: will the company you built for so many years be valued the way you hope? That fear is real, and it is the one founders talk about.

The less obvious one matters more. I often tell advisors that a founder can believe they have made the decision to sell, and point to the fact that they are working with an exit advisor as proof, while the decision is in fact still open. They haven't fully committed to it.

When that happens, founders start putting roadblocks into the process, often without being aware of it. Underneath it is usually fear: not knowing who they will be, or what they will do, once the company is no longer theirs. So part of them pulls back, even while the rest of them feels sure that selling is right. It is a tricky place to be, because from the inside it doesn't feel like doubt. It feels like being careful.

## During the sale: what happens when the business is picked apart?

Due diligence is a buyer's job. They need to question the numbers, test the assumptions and look for risk. From their side it makes complete sense.

From the founder's side, it can feel personal. Every question about the business lands as a question about you, and the natural response is to justify and prove yourself. That is understandable, and it rarely helps.

What helps is staying neutral: answering clearly without defending, and not getting too attached to any single outcome, including the price. That is difficult when the business has been your life's work. It is also a skill that can be practiced, and it is a large part of what we help founders with at DoGood.

## How do a founder's emotions affect the deal?

I'm not at the table when deals are negotiated. But advisors tell me, often, that they have lost deals because of the founder's emotions. The pattern is familiar: the closer the deal gets to signing, the harder the founder pulls on the brakes.

That is why the work we do with founders before a sale helps their advisors too. A founder who has looked at their fears is less likely to stall the process without knowing it. We cover this side in more depth in [Why deals stall when the founder isn't ready](/insights/why-deals-stall-when-the-founder-isnt-ready).

## After closing: relief, resentment, guilt and grief

The first feeling after closing is usually relief. The process is over, and the weight of it lifts.

Other feelings follow, often at the same time:

- **Resentment**, if the final price wasn't the one you wanted.
- **Guilt** toward the staff and suppliers you feel you have let down, and who now depend on someone else.
- **Grief** for the company that is no longer yours. It may not come in the first week or two, but it soon arrives, and when it hits, it hits.

None of these means the sale was a mistake. They mean something important has ended.

## Is that grief the same as losing your identity?

They are related, but they are not the same. The grief is about the loss of the company itself: the thing you built, the people, the place it had in your life.

Identity loss is internal. It is not knowing who you are without the company, and not knowing what to do next. The sense of purpose that organized your days has gone, and nothing has replaced it yet. We write about this side in [Sudden wealth syndrome after selling your business](/insights/sudden-wealth-syndrome-after-selling-a-business).

It helps to tell the two apart, because they need different things. Grief needs time and room. The identity question needs active work on who you are becoming.

## What should you do with these feelings?

Be aware of them. Let them be there. And work with someone who knows how to handle them.

That sounds simple, and it is. The difficulty is that founders are used to solving things alone and pushing through. Pushing through works for a product launch. It doesn't work for an ending like this.
