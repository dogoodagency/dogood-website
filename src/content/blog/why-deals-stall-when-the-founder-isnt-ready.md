## Why do deals stall when the founder isn't ready?

I speak to advisors all the time, and one thing comes up again and again: the founder didn't just stall the deal, they tanked it. I'm not at the negotiating table, so I can't tell you the details of individual deals. But among the advisors I talk to, this is close to a consensus.

The reason is rarely the numbers. It is that the founder is not emotionally ready to stay calm under stress and let their baby go. For most founders this is their first exit. They have never been through one before, and nothing about building a company prepares them for handing it over.

## What does it look like from the advisor's side?

The founder starts arguing with the advisor. They push back. They get angry. They become more and more emotional the closer it gets to the exit date.

It can happen at any stage of the deal. It depends on the deal, the founder, the setup and the situation. What stays the same is the direction: the closer the deal gets to done, the harder the founder pulls on the brakes.

## Personal fear or real concern: how can you tell?

Founders have every right to raise concerns about the price, the buyer or the terms, and some concerns should slow a deal down. The difference is in how they show up.

A real concern is a calm conversation. The founder can say clearly what the problem is and why it matters.

A personal fear is usually loud. It shows up as shouting and anger, a kind of emotional constriction. Underneath it is often the fear of not knowing who they will be once the company is no longer theirs.

Once you look for it, the difference is very clear.

## Why is it so hard to raise with a client?

It is a tricky situation. Some founders are simply not ready to hear that they might want support.

In my experience, the founders who need it most are usually the ones you can't talk to about it. The founders who are managing a bit better tend to be more self-aware. They ask for help themselves, or they open up to their advisor emotionally, and that opens the door.

That is one more reason to raise it early, before the pressure of the deal makes the conversation harder.

## What makes it worse?

Taking it personally. When a founder gets emotional, it is natural to defend your work, and the conversation gets inflamed. That rarely helps either side.

It helps to recognize what is happening. This is the emotional side of a founder who hasn't been through many deals and isn't equipped to deal with it yet. It is an emotional imbalance, if you will, not a verdict on your work.

## When should you bring in support, and how does it work?

As early as possible, ideally 12 to 18 months before the exit.

We work in close collaboration with the founder and the advisor. If the founder gives us room within our confidentiality agreement, we also give the advisor feedback on how the founder is doing on the personal side. What we share always depends on what the founder has agreed to.

We work only on the personal side of the exit. You stay the trusted advisor.

## What changes when the founder has done the personal work?

The founder remains calm, collected and neutral. They stay on point, they see the bigger picture, and they see more in life than just the company.

A stalled deal costs a lot of time, money and relationships, all of it. Preparing the founder early is how you avoid it.
