## Why the first 90 days are simpler than the checklists suggest

Search for what to do after selling a business and you will find long plans: three phases, dozens of tasks, tables of weekly routines. Most professionals overcomplicate this stretch.

The money side does need attention, and that work belongs with your tax advisor and wealth advisor. The personal side is simple. It is not easy, but it is simple: rest first, then reflect, and don't rush.

On the money side, the first 90 days usually mean setting aside what you owe in tax, keeping the proceeds somewhere safe while you decide what to do with them, and understanding any earn-out or handover obligations in the sale agreement. Your advisors will lead on all of that. What they won't do is ask how you are, who you are becoming, or what you want your days to look like. That part is yours, and it is what this article is about.

## Months one and two: celebrate, rest, recover

You have just finished one of the biggest projects of your life. Celebrate it. Then rest.

Take care of yourself first. Most founders have spent years putting the company ahead of their health, their sleep and the people close to them. The first weeks after a sale are the time to give that back to yourself.

In practice that can be very simple. Sleep without an alarm. Book the health check you kept postponing. Move your body every day. Spend unhurried time with the people who carried the cost of the company alongside you. None of it needs a plan or a target.

A holiday is fine. Treating yourself is fine. What matters is that these things don't become a way to avoid what comes next. Many founders distract themselves with travel and buying things, and the reflection that should follow never happens.

## Month three: reflect and get clear

Around month three, start to look back and look ahead. What worked over the years of building the company? What would you never repeat? What did it cost you? And what does it mean to step into the next version of yourself, the one you actually want to create?

Some questions worth sitting with in month three:

- What did I love about building the company, and what did I only tolerate?
- Which of my habits served the company but not me?
- What did the company cost me, and who else paid that price?
- What am I no longer willing to do, whatever it pays?
- If I didn't need to prove anything to anyone, what would I want my days to look like?

Write the answers down. You don't need to act on them yet. You need to see them clearly.

This step matters because of patterns. Founders who skip it and go straight into a new venture carry the old patterns with them, the same ones that created the problems last time. The new company ends up looking a lot like the old one.

## Why it often hits later than you expect

Many founders feel fine in the first month. The deal is done, there is relief, and often a celebration. The quiet tends to arrive around month three, when the congratulations stop and the calendar stays empty: the first ordinary Monday with nothing in it. For some it comes later still, around the one-year mark, when a handover ends or the next venture doesn't feel the way they hoped.

If that happens to you, nothing has gone wrong. It is a common pattern, and it is the reason the reflection in month three matters.

## The empty calendar: what replaces the structure?

Founders need routine and structure, and after a sale both are usually missing. The company took up so much space for so long that nothing is ready to fill it.

Some founders can sit in that space and use it to pause, reflect and recharge. Most can't, at least not at first. For them the empty calendar becomes a struggle, and the temptation is to fill it with random things that mean very little to them, just to feel busy again.

I know this from the inside. When I lost my company, I was living in Bali. It was 2024, I was surrounded by nature and beauty, and I had more time than I had ever had in my life. It was a real struggle. I sat in a silence that felt like it was eating me alive, and everything felt meaningless. What helped in the end was not filling the silence but learning to be in it: becoming familiar with it, then comfortable with it. That is where the clarity eventually came from.

If the empty calendar feels unbearable, that is normal. Resist the urge to fill it with whatever comes along.

A few fixed anchors help: time outdoors, regular exercise, a standing time each week with people you care about. They give the week a shape without filling it. The rest of the space is there for rest and reflection, even when that feels uncomfortable.

## Staying on after the sale: why no hard cuts

If you have the option, a smooth handover is better than a hard cut. I am in favor of founders staying on the board, or staying involved in the company, for the first 90 days or even six months after the sale.

A gradual exit gives the team, the customers and the buyer continuity. It also gives you time to let go step by step, instead of losing your role, your routine and your people on the same day.

If you do stay on, be clear about the terms from the start: what your role is, how long it lasts, and what you are no longer responsible for. Agree it with the buyer and get it into the deal with your advisors. Staying on without clear boundaries can make letting go harder, not easier.

## What changes at home

After a sale, most founders suddenly spend far more time at home. That can be wonderful. It can also bring partner or family issues to the surface that have been set aside, sometimes for years, while the company came first.

That is not a sign that something has gone wrong. It is part of the transition, and it needs as much attention as the money and the next venture.

Talk about it early. What does each of you expect from this new time together? What has been put off that now needs attention? These conversations are easier in the first month than in the sixth.

## Common mistakes in the first 90 days

Watch for these:

- Rushing into a new venture and carrying the old patterns straight into it.
- Using holidays and purchases to avoid reflection, rather than to rest.
- Filling the empty calendar with things that mean nothing to them.
- Treating the transition as one more project to optimize.
- Cutting all ties overnight instead of letting go step by step.
- Ignoring what is surfacing at home.

## Day 90: how do you know you're on track?

By the end of the first 90 days, a founder who is on track can clearly say:

- who they are now, without the company
- what has changed in them since the sale
- what they did wrong in the past, and what they don't want to step into again
- what specifically excites them about what comes next, as a vision for themselves

And one more thing: they have let go of the company. If you are still checking on it, still running it in your head, or still measuring yourself by it, there is more work to do, and that is fine.
