## Why preparing yourself is different from preparing the company

Search for how to prepare to sell your business and almost every result says the same things. Clean up three to five years of financials. Get a valuation. Build your deal team. Reduce customer concentration. Make the business run without you.

All of that matters, and good advisors will take you through it. But notice who it is for. Even "make yourself redundant" is advice for the buyer's benefit, so the business is worth more without you in it.

Almost nobody prepares the founder. Yet the founder is the one who has to sign, live through due diligence, hand over the company and then wake up the next morning without it. A well-prepared company with an unprepared founder is a risk to the deal and to the founder.

## What does preparing yourself actually mean?

It means taking time to think, honestly and in some depth, about questions most founders have not had room for in years:

- Who do you want to be without the company?
- What is genuinely important to you now?
- What have you been ignoring for the last few years, or decades?
- What brings back the sense of fun that so many founders have lost along the way?
- Which relationships do you want to nurture next?
- What becomes meaningful to you once the company is no longer there?
- What legacy do you want to leave beyond the company?
- What does your health need from you?

Underneath all of them is one bigger question: how do you want to step forward into a version of yourself that you are truly inspired by? Not a version the company needed, or one that other people expect, but one you feel energized by.

## Why it takes 12 to 18 months

None of these questions has a straight answer. You don't sit down one afternoon, write the answers and move on.

It is a process: discovery, reflection, drafting, then going back over what you drafted. It works like peeling an onion. You go around and around, and each time a layer comes off, until you find the core. The first answers founders give are usually the ones they think they should give. The real ones come later.

That is why the time matters. Twelve to eighteen months before the exit gives you room to go through several layers while you still have the structure of the company around you, rather than starting from nothing after the deal has closed.

## How do you know you're personally ready to sell?

One sign you are ready is a sense of "meh". The excitement has gone. The drive has gone. But it is important to look at why.

If it is burnout, you are exhausted, and rest might bring the drive back. Readiness is different. The company is simply no longer aligned with who you are, or with who you want to be. You could keep going, but it no longer feels like yours to carry.

A sign you are not ready is a life that is constantly being filled with new things: new projects, new commitments, new distractions. Founders who do this are often avoiding the real question of who they want to be without the company. As long as the calendar is full, they don't have to answer it.

## Stepping back before the sale

Exit advisors spend a lot of time on owner dependency: how much the business relies on you personally. Reducing it raises the value of the company, and it does something else too.

It teaches the company independence. Your leadership team has to step up to the plate and make decisions without you. That is a real leadership skill, and it has to be trained and embodied, not just written into an organization chart. The earlier it starts, the more natural it is by the time a buyer is looking at it.

## How clear do you need to be about what's next?

Clearer than most founders expect. A vague sense of "I'll travel, then see" is not enough.

A founder who is ready can explain what comes next in detail, and gets emotionally involved and excited while talking about it, without the company being part of the picture. That last part is where many founders struggle. When you listen closely, a lot of them are still attached to the idea of running the company, or of running the next one. The next chapter they describe is the old chapter with a new name.

## The personal leg: working alongside your advisors

Exit planners often describe readiness as a three-legged stool: the business, your finances and you personally. The first two legs are well served.

Exit advisors, succession planners, family offices, wealth advisors and sell-side M&A advisors already do a great deal. Some exit planners work on owner dependency, leadership and a more diversified client mix. Some prepare the whole transaction, and some also bring in the buyer. That is a lot of work, and it is important work.

What none of them has is help with the personal side. That is what we specialize in at DoGood, and we work alongside these advisors, not instead of them. In our view it is not optional. Without the personal leg, the stool doesn't stand.

## What does a well-prepared founder look like after the sale?

You can see it. Founders who did this work before signing are calm after the sale. They are content. They enjoy the moment they are in, and they are grateful for it.

They also have a clear vision of who they want to become next, without being frantic about it, and without the fear of loss that drives so many founders straight into the next venture.

## You can't prepare for everything

I want to be honest about one thing. You can never fully prepare for this. When I lost my company, it was a smack in the face. Nothing I had planned covered it, and it is not something I would wish on anyone.

It also taught me valuable lessons, and I carry them into my next chapter of entrepreneurship and into how I serve the founders we work with. Preparation doesn't remove the shock. It means the shock lands on someone who already knows what matters to them.
